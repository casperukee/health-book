# Children And Adolescent Health: Safety, Function, And Support

> This page is not medical advice. Children and teens need age-appropriate clinical care. Breathing trouble, altered consciousness, seizures, serious injury, dehydration, severe pain, self-harm or suicide risk, abuse or violence risk, or severe behavioral change should move quickly to emergency, pediatric, mental health crisis, or local professional support.

[中文验收页](../../../en-review/zh-CN/book/part-2-body-risk-map/children-and-adolescent-health.md)

A child says their stomach hurts before school. A teen cannot fall asleep, then cannot wake up. A daughter worries about periods, acne, weight, or body shape. A son closes the door, stops talking, argues more, and disappears into a screen. Grades drop. The first adult explanation arrives fast: phone, attitude, puberty, laziness, anxiety, school pressure.

Sometimes those explanations touch part of the truth. They are still too fast.

For children and adolescents, health is not only "sick or not sick," and not only "behaving or misbehaving." It is a developing person trying to stay safe, keep daily life functioning, handle body changes, and stay connected to people who can help.

## Children Are Not Small Adults

Adults can usually describe symptoms with time, location, triggers, and severity. Children may only say "my stomach hurts," "I'm tired," "I don't know," "leave me alone," or "I don't want to go."

The same sentence can sit near infection, allergy, poor sleep, vision problems, menstrual pain, sports injury, anxiety, bullying, family conflict, online harm, or several things at once. Younger children often speak through body complaints. Teens may mix body, identity, privacy, peers, and self-worth into one difficult knot.

Two mistakes are common:

- moralizing a body problem: "You are just trying to skip school";
- medicalizing every developmental change: "This mood means something is wrong with you."

A better first move is to see the child as developing. Development means many changes are normal for age, but the ability to explain symptoms, manage risk, and seek help is still incomplete. Adults hold the safety boundary while slowly giving the young person more participation.

## Use Four Lines

When a child or teen changes, look across four lines:

| Line | Family question |
| --- | --- |
| Safety | Is there any immediate danger, self-harm risk, violence, abuse, serious symptom, or situation that cannot wait? |
| Body | What symptoms, sleep, appetite, growth, pain, periods, vision, injuries, medicines, or substance exposures are relevant? |
| Function | Can the child sleep, wake, eat, attend school, do basic tasks, move, learn, and keep some daily rhythm? |
| Relationships | What is happening with family, peers, school, online spaces, trusted adults, and belonging? |

Safety and body come first, but do not stop there. A child can have real body symptoms and real stress at the same time.

## Protect The Health Floor

Families cannot control every detail of a young person's life, and trying to do so often makes health information disappear. The better target is the health floor:

- enough sleep for the age and situation;
- regular movement, outdoor time, or a physical activity the child can actually keep doing;
- a food environment that does not rely on shame about weight or appearance;
- basic pediatric, dental, vision, menstrual, injury, allergy, and mental health care when needed;
- clear safety rules about self-harm, violence, abuse, substances, dangerous online content, and driving or other age-specific risks;
- growing privacy and participation as the child becomes a teen.

Shame is a poor health tool. Do not mock body odor, acne, weight, puberty timing, periods, masturbation, sexual curiosity, voice changes, or appearance worries. Body changes can be awkward without being dirty or dangerous. Body problems can be private without being unspeakable.

## Function Matters More Than Labels

A teen can be moody, private, and intense without needing a diagnosis. A teen can also be in real trouble while adults keep explaining it as "normal puberty."

Look at function:

- sleep is collapsing;
- school attendance or work is falling apart;
- eating changes sharply;
- hygiene, movement, or daily tasks are slipping;
- the young person is isolated from almost everyone;
- there is persistent distress, panic, anger, sadness, fear, or numbness;
- the family feels the child cannot safely be left alone.

NIMH encourages families to seek help when a child's mood or behavior lasts for weeks, causes distress, or interferes with school, home, or friends. Immediate help is needed when behavior is unsafe or the child talks about harming themselves or others.

In family language: do not rush every mood into a diagnosis, and do not keep lecturing when function and safety are already failing.

## Screens Are Often A Signal, Not The Whole Cause

Phones, games, and short videos can worsen sleep, movement, attention, eye strain, social pressure, online conflict, privacy risk, and exposure to harmful content. Limits may be necessary.

But if every problem is blamed on the phone, the family may miss the reason the phone became the only relief.

Ask:

1. Is screen time displacing sleep, school, meals, movement, or family contact?
2. Is there bullying, harassment, sexual content, violence, scams, gambling-like spending, or dangerous challenges?
3. Does the online world make the child more connected or more trapped?
4. What offline space gives the child competence, belonging, privacy, or calm?
5. Which adult can the child tell something hard to without being immediately punished or shamed?

Taking away the phone without adding sleep, movement, support, connection, and safety may only move the distress somewhere else.

## Talk In Smaller Doorways

Questions that start with "why" can sound like a trial:

- "Why won't you go to school?"
- "Why are you always on your phone?"
- "What's wrong with you?"

Try "which step" instead:

- "Morning, leaving home, entering school, class, lunch, or seeing certain people: which part feels hardest?"
- "What does the phone help you escape from?"
- "What time of day feels worst?"
- "Is there anyone or anything at school or online that makes you feel unsafe, ashamed, or trapped?"

If the child says a little, hold it before correcting it. A useful first response is: "Thank you for telling me. I am not going to solve everything in one minute. First we make sure you are safe, then we decide the next step."

If the child will not talk, leave a safety doorway open: "You do not have to explain everything now. But if you might hurt yourself, hurt someone else, be hurt by someone, or cannot stay safe, we have to bring in help."

## When Not To Keep Guessing At Home

Move quickly toward emergency, pediatric urgent care, the emergency department, crisis services, school emergency support, or another local professional entry point when there is breathing trouble, blue lips, altered consciousness, seizures, serious head injury, severe dehydration, severe or worsening pain, uncontrolled bleeding, suspected fracture, severe allergic reaction, poisoning, or sudden neurologic symptoms.

Mental health and safety red flags include suicidal thoughts, self-harm, threats to harm others, access to means, farewell messages, abuse, sexual assault, serious bullying or threats, hallucinations, delusions, extreme confusion, severe agitation, days with almost no sleep plus unsafe behavior, or a caregiver's strong sense that the child cannot be left alone tonight.

In the U.S., 988 is a crisis resource for suicide, mental health, and substance-use crisis support. Use 911 or emergency care when there is immediate danger. Outside the U.S., use local emergency and crisis resources.

## Make A One-Week Change Note

If there is no immediate red flag, write one week of simple notes:

- when the change started;
- body symptoms, sleep, appetite, periods, injury, illness, medicines, or substances;
- school attendance, homework, hygiene, movement, and social contact;
- mood, behavior, online conflict, peer issues, family stress, or bullying concerns;
- the child's own words;
- what makes things better or worse.

Do not wait for a perfect record if safety changes. But for pediatric, school, counseling, psychiatry, gynecology, sports medicine, vision, or other care, a one-page timeline is far better than "they have been different lately."
