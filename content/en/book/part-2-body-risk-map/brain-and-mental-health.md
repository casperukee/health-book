# Brain And Mental Health: Start With Safety, Function, And Support

> This page is not medical advice. Stroke-like symptoms, major confusion, self-harm risk, suicide risk, psychosis, severe depression or anxiety, sudden cognitive or behavior change, or inability to stay safe need urgent professional help.

[中文验收页](../../../en-review/zh-CN/book/part-2-body-risk-map/brain-and-mental-health.md)

Families often meet brain and mental health problems before they have a name.

Someone has weeks of poor sleep, panic, stomach discomfort, and repeated checking. An older parent misses medications, leaves the stove on, sends money to a stranger, or gets lost on a familiar route. A teenager stops going to school, stays in the room, sleeps badly, and says life feels pointless.

The first useful move is not to decide the diagnosis. It is to ask four questions:

1. Is this safe right now?
2. What daily function has changed?
3. What pattern has been building?
4. What support can be connected?

These questions keep a family from two common mistakes: dismissing real change as personality, age, or weakness; or turning every change into a label.

## Safety Comes First

Use emergency or crisis pathways when there are:

- sudden face drooping, one-sided weakness or numbness, speech trouble, vision change, severe sudden headache, trouble walking, dizziness, loss of balance, seizure, fainting, or major confusion;
- thoughts, plans, tools, or actions related to self-harm, suicide, or harming someone else;
- hallucinations, delusions, extreme agitation, inability to sleep for a long time with unsafe behavior, or loss of contact with reality;
- sudden behavior or cognitive change in an older adult, especially after infection, dehydration, fall, medication change, surgery, alcohol/substance use, or illness.

In the U.S. and Canada, call or text 988 for suicidal crisis, mental health crisis, or substance-use crisis support. Call 911 or go to the emergency department when there is immediate danger, possible stroke, severe confusion, serious injury, or inability to keep someone safe.

## Function Matters More Than Labels

Low mood, worry, forgetfulness, and stress happen to everyone. They become harder to dismiss when they change daily function.

Track what has changed:

- sleep, appetite, energy, attention, school, work, caregiving, social contact;
- medication use, cooking, driving, money handling, scams, appointments, hygiene;
- fear, avoidance, repeated checking, panic, compulsive behaviors, substance use;
- interest, pleasure, hopelessness, guilt, worthlessness, irritability, anger, numbness;
- memory, language, judgment, orientation, balance, falls, wandering, or unsafe decisions.

Do not write "probably depression" or "probably dementia" as the only note. Write what happened: "missed pills three times this week," "left stove on," "has not gone to school for nine days," "searched symptoms for hours every night," "said everyone would be better off without me."

Specific facts help clinicians. Labels from the family often do not.

## Mental Health Is Not A Character Verdict

Depression is not always crying. It may look like numbness, exhaustion, slowed movement, irritability, lost interest, poor concentration, sleep or appetite change, guilt, hopelessness, or thoughts of death.

Anxiety is not always "too much thinking." It may look like panic symptoms, repeated reassurance-seeking, avoidance, checking, physical tension, stomach symptoms, insomnia, or fear that keeps shrinking life.

The body still matters. Chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, neurologic symptoms, severe pain, thyroid disease, anemia, medication effects, alcohol/substance use, sleep apnea, and chronic illness can overlap with mood and anxiety symptoms. Do not use "stress" as a shortcut to ignore medical red flags.

A better family sentence is:

> This is real distress, and we need to understand both body signals and function.

Professional help can include primary care, therapy, psychiatry, school support, crisis services, social work, or specialty evaluation. It is not a failure of family support.

## Cognitive Change Is A Function Story

For older adults, memory change is not all the same.

Occasionally misplacing keys or taking longer to recall a name can happen with normal aging. But changes that affect medication safety, cooking, driving, finances, scams, language, route-finding, judgment, hygiene, or social life deserve attention.

Record:

- what changed and when;
- whether it was sudden or gradual;
- whether there were falls, infection, dehydration, medication changes, sleep problems, alcohol/substance use, hospitalization, surgery, or stroke-like symptoms;
- whether the person can still manage medications, money, meals, appointments, phone use, transportation, and home safety;
- what family members, coworkers, teachers, or friends have noticed.

Sudden confusion is not "normal aging." It needs medical evaluation.

## How To Talk Without Turning Care Into Control

Start with observations, not accusations:

- "I noticed you have not been sleeping and you stopped going to class. I am worried about safety and support."
- "Dad, this month there were three medication mistakes and one stove incident. I want us to bring facts to the doctor."
- "I am not here to argue whether this is mental or physical. I want to help you get through the next step."
- "If you might hurt yourself, I am staying with you and calling for help."

Support is not becoming the person's therapist or detective. Support means reducing shame, staying connected, helping with appointments, organizing facts, and escalating when safety is at risk.

## A One-Week Brain And Mood Card

Use this for yourself or someone you care about:

```text
Safety: any self-harm, suicide, harm-to-others, stroke-like symptoms, confusion, or psychosis?
Function: sleep, eating, school/work, caregiving, medications, money, driving, hygiene, social contact:
Pattern: when did this start, sudden or gradual, better or worse?
Body context: pain, infection, fall, medications, alcohol/substance use, sleep, chronic disease:
Support: who knows, who can help, what appointment or crisis step is needed?
```

Bring the card to primary care, mental health care, neurology, geriatrics, school support, or crisis services as appropriate.

## When Not To Keep Observing

Do not wait when:

- there are suicidal thoughts, plans, tools, attempts, goodbye messages, or preparations;
- there is possible stroke, seizure, major confusion, sudden weakness, speech trouble, or severe sudden headache;
- depression, anxiety, panic, insomnia, eating change, or substance use is impairing basic life;
- a child or teenager talks about not wanting to live, self-harm, harming others, or feels unsafe;
- an older adult has sudden confusion, unsafe behavior, falls, wandering, or rapid functional decline;
- hallucinations, delusions, extreme agitation, or inability to sleep with unsafe behavior appears.

For brain and mental health, do not start with a label. Start with safety, function, pattern, and support.
