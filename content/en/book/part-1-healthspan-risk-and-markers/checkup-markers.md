# Checkup Markers Are Risk Language

> This page is not medical advice. A lab report, imaging result, screening result, or portal flag cannot diagnose or rule out disease by itself. Symptoms, history, repeated measurements, medications, and clinician judgment matter.

[中文验收页](../../../en-review/zh-CN/book/part-1-healthspan-risk-and-markers/checkup-markers.md)

A checkup report can turn a calm day into a family debate. A patient portal shows a red flag. Someone searches the abbreviation. Someone else says it was "almost the same last year." A normal-looking page gets treated like proof that everything is fine.

The report rarely answers the question families actually have:

> What should we do next?

Read checkup markers as risk language. The important question is not one arrow. It is who the result belongs to, whether it is new or persistent, what other markers point in the same direction, and what action should change.

## First Sort The Report Into Three Buckets

| Bucket | Meaning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Urgent symptoms | Chest pain, possible stroke, severe breathing trouble, fainting, major confusion, uncontrolled bleeding, severe pain, self-harm risk, or other red flags | Do not use a checkup report to reassure yourself. Use urgent care pathways. |
| Clinician follow-up | Clearly abnormal, persistent, changing, grouped, or high-risk results | Prepare facts and contact a clinician for interpretation and next steps. |
| Record and watch | Mild, isolated, improving, or context-dependent findings with no warning signs | Record the result, compare with prior reports, and ask when to repeat or follow up. |

If symptoms and the report disagree, symptoms can matter more. A normal report does not make chest pain or stroke-like symptoms safe.

## Look For Patterns Before Arrows

Many markers belong to risk lines.

| Risk line | Common markers or clues | How to think about them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cardiovascular and metabolic | blood pressure, lipids, glucose, A1C, weight, waist, triglycerides | Look at the pattern and overall risk, not one number alone |
| Kidney and urine | creatinine, estimated GFR, urine protein or albumin, blood in urine, blood pressure | Interpret with history, diabetes, blood pressure, medications, hydration, and repeat testing |
| Liver, alcohol, medication, and metabolic clues | ALT, AST, bilirubin, albumin, fatty liver wording | Ask whether this is persistent, medication-related, alcohol-related, metabolic, viral, or needs follow-up |
| Blood count and inflammation clues | white blood cells, hemoglobin, platelets | Context matters: infection, bleeding, chronic disease, medications, and repeat values |
| Screening and imaging | mammogram, cervical screening, colorectal screening, lung cancer screening for high-risk people, nodules, cysts, tumor markers | Ask what follow-up interval or specialist review is recommended |

The same LDL cholesterol, A1C, blood pressure, creatinine, or imaging word can mean different things in different people.

## Use Four Lenses

### 1. Trend

Is this the first abnormal result, or has it appeared repeatedly? Is it slowly drifting, suddenly changing, or returning to a prior pattern?

One abnormal value may reflect timing, illness, dehydration, recent exercise, medication, lab variation, or a real signal. Trend does not answer everything, but it prevents overreacting to one number and underreacting to repeated change.

### 2. Combination

Markers often speak together.

- high blood pressure plus abnormal lipids raises cardiovascular and kidney questions;
- glucose/A1C plus waist gain plus high triglycerides suggests a metabolic pattern;
- kidney markers plus blood pressure plus diabetes history need to be interpreted together;
- anemia plus weight loss, bleeding, fatigue, or abnormal stool should not be treated like a random lab oddity.

### 3. Context

Ask what was happening around the test:

- fasting or not;
- recent infection, dehydration, heavy exercise, alcohol, poor sleep, stress, menstrual bleeding, pregnancy/postpartum, or injury;
- new prescription, over-the-counter medicine, supplement, or herbal product;
- known chronic disease, family history, smoking, or recent hospitalization.

Context does not erase abnormal results. It helps decide whether to repeat, follow up, or escalate.

### 4. Action

Every important marker should lead to a practical question:

- Should this be repeated?
- Should I bring prior reports?
- Which markers belong together?
- What symptoms should trigger earlier care?
- What should I track before the next visit?
- Does this change lifestyle work, medication discussion, referral, screening, or follow-up?

If a result creates anxiety but no clear next step, ask a clinician what the result is for.

## Six Common Mistakes

1. **Treating the reference range as a diagnosis line.** A value outside the reference range is not automatically a disease; a value inside it is not proof of no risk.
2. **Turning one result into a life story.** A single abnormal or normal result is not a long-term trend.
3. **Reading one marker alone.** Blood pressure, lipids, glucose, kidney markers, body weight, waist, and medications often need to be interpreted together.
4. **Using checkups instead of care for symptoms.** A checkup is not an emergency evaluation, clinic visit, or specialist assessment.
5. **Buying more tests for reassurance.** More screening is not always better. Good screening depends on evidence, target population, follow-up pathway, and harms as well as benefits.
6. **Changing medication or supplements on your own.** A result can be a reason to ask a clinician or pharmacist; it should not become a reason to stop, start, or mix treatments without guidance.

## A 20-Minute Report Review

Before sending screenshots to everyone, make a short summary:

```text
Main abnormal results:
Prior results from the last 1-3 reports:
Symptoms or red flags:
Recent context: illness, sleep, alcohol, exercise, medications, supplements, stress:
Questions for the clinician:
1.
2.
3.
```

Example:

```text
Main abnormal results: LDL and triglycerides flagged; waist increased.
Prior results: LDL was high last year and higher this year.
Symptoms: no chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, or stroke-like symptoms.
Recent context: less sleep, less exercise, weight gain.
Questions: What is my overall cardiovascular risk? When should I repeat labs? What should make me seek care earlier?
```

This is not self-diagnosis. It turns a report into a useful visit conversation.

## Screening Is Not A Shopping List

Preventive screening can save lives when it is used for the right people, at the right interval, with a clear follow-up pathway. It can also create false positives, overdiagnosis, anxiety, costs, and procedures that may not help.

Before choosing a checkup package or extra test, ask:

- Is this recommended for someone my age, sex, risk factors, and history?
- What happens if it is positive?
- What happens if it is unclear?
- What are the harms of false positives or overdiagnosis?
- Will the result change what I do?

Tumor markers, whole-body imaging, multi-cancer blood tests, genetic tests, and direct-to-consumer tests need special caution. They may have valid uses, but they are not universal shortcuts to safety.

For choosing tests before a visit, use [Before a Checkup](../../handbook/playbooks/checkup-planning-guide.md).

## When To Bring It To A Clinician

Use professional judgment when:

- a result is clearly abnormal, persistent, or changing quickly;
- several cardiovascular, metabolic, kidney, liver, or blood markers are abnormal together;
- the person has diabetes, high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, cancer, immune disease, pregnancy/postpartum, older age, childhood, or complex medications;
- abnormal results come with chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, confusion, one-sided weakness, speech trouble, severe pain, abnormal bleeding, or self-harm risk;
- the report says positive, suspicious, mass, needs further evaluation, or specialist follow-up, and you do not know the next step.

Positive or suspicious does not always mean a final diagnosis. It usually means the result needs confirmation, context, or follow-up.

A checkup marker is not a verdict. It is a clue that becomes useful only when you connect trend, combination, context, and action.
