# 3. Checkup Markers: Not A Verdict, But A Risk Language

> This page is not medical advice. Checkup reports, lab results, screening results, and imaging findings need to be interpreted by a clinician together with symptoms, history, physical findings, prior results, and other tests. Do not use this page to diagnose yourself, change medication, stop medication, or delay care.

For many families, a checkup report is the first place where the risk curve becomes visible.

The first reaction is often not reading, but screenshotting. The image goes into a family chat, and the red arrow looks like an alarm. Someone searches the abbreviation, someone asks "is this serious," someone says "don't scare yourself, it was almost the same last year," and someone treats the page with no arrows as a pass certificate.

But the report usually does not answer the question that makes people anxious: what should we do next?

A red arrow may be a one-time fluctuation, or it may be an early signal of long-term risk. A normal-looking page cannot explain chest pain, fainting, sudden speech trouble, or ongoing weight loss and fatigue. A report gives clues, not a final verdict. It becomes useful only when it is placed next to symptoms, age, family history, prior disease, medicines, lifestyle, and previous results.

So after you receive a report, do not search every abbreviation first. If there are no dangerous symptoms, the first question is not "which arrow is scariest?" It is whether these numbers are speaking about the same problem.

Markers are not verdicts. They are risk language. The key is not one arrow, but who it appears in, how long it has been present, what appears with it, and what action changes.

## Look At The Combination Before The Arrow

Most arrows in a checkup report can first be placed on a few risk lines.

| Risk line | Common markers | First page to read |
|---|---|---|
| Cardiovascular and metabolic | blood pressure, lipids, glucose, A1C, uric acid, weight, waist | [Blood pressure](../../handbook/playbooks/common-checkup-markers.md#blood-pressure), [lipids](../../handbook/playbooks/common-checkup-markers.md#blood-lipids), [glucose/A1C](../../handbook/playbooks/common-checkup-markers.md#blood-glucose-a1c), [uric acid](../../handbook/playbooks/common-checkup-markers.md#uric-acid) |
| Kidney | creatinine, eGFR, urine protein, urine albumin, blood in urine | [Kidney function and urine tests](../../handbook/playbooks/common-checkup-markers.md#kidney-urine) |
| Liver, gallbladder, and medication safety | ALT, AST, bilirubin, albumin, fatty liver wording | [Liver function](../../handbook/playbooks/common-checkup-markers.md#liver-function) |
| Blood and infection clues | white blood cells, hemoglobin, platelets, and related CBC markers | [Blood count](../../handbook/playbooks/common-checkup-markers.md#blood-count) |
| Cancer screening and imaging | breast, cervical, colorectal, high-risk lung cancer screening, nodules, tumor markers | [Tumor markers and imaging findings](../../handbook/playbooks/common-checkup-markers.md#tumor-imaging), and for screening choices see [Before A Checkup](../../handbook/playbooks/checkup-planning-guide.md) |

What deserves attention is often not "one arrow," but "several risk lines pointing in the same direction."

For example:

- high blood pressure plus abnormal lipids is not only about less salt and oil; it is about heart attack, stroke, and kidney risk;
- borderline glucose plus increasing waist plus high triglycerides is not only about whether diabetes has been diagnosed; the metabolic system may already be tightening;
- high uric acid plus high blood pressure plus borderline kidney markers is not only about seafood and beer; kidney clearance, weight, alcohol, and medicines need to be read together;
- diabetes plus abnormal blood pressure or lipids is not only about glucose; cardiovascular, kidney, eye, nerve, and foot complications all need prevention.

An easier image is this: two people post checkup reports in the family chat at the same time.

The first person has one mildly high lipid value, and the red arrow is eye-catching, so the whole family stares at that line. The second person's report looks less dramatic, but they are older, and blood pressure, glucose, waist, smoking history, and family history are all accumulating in the same direction. To a clinician, the second person may not be "lighter." The real question is not whose arrow is redder, but whose overall risk is higher, whether it has worsened over several years, and whether the next step is recording, repeat testing, a clinical conversation, or further evaluation.

Glucose is similar. One normal fasting glucose does not mean the whole glucose system is under no stress; one high value also should not be diagnosed without considering the previous day's food, sleep, infection, stress, medicines, and prior results. A report is a window, not the whole movie.

If these combinations keep appearing, do not only debate in the family chat about "what to eat to lower it." A better action is to put them into the [Chronic Marker Log](../../handbook/templates/family-health-record.md#_2-chronic-marker-log) and bring the trend to follow-up.

## First Sort Into Three Categories In 30 Seconds

Sort into three categories first:

- **Urgent:** when chest pain, stroke-like symptoms, severe breathing trouble, fainting, altered consciousness, abnormal bleeding, severe pain, self-harm risk, or other warning signs are present, a normal report does not make waiting safe.
- **Clinic or repeat-test issue:** clear abnormalities, persistent abnormalities, or clustered abnormalities should be organized and discussed with a clinician.
- **Record-and-watch issue:** one mild abnormality should first be checked against whether it is new, what changed recently, and whether prior results looked similar.

The point of this step is to lower the emotional temperature. When a report arrives, people tend to swing between two extremes: "I am doomed" or "no arrows means everything is fine." Sorting into three categories helps you see what must be handled now, what belongs with a clinician, and what should first become a record.

For warning signs, use [Red Flags](../../handbook/playbooks/red-flags.md). For symptom next steps, use [Symptom Action Guide](../../handbook/playbooks/symptom-action-guide.md). If the issue is only that the report is hard to understand, keep reading.

## The 20 Minutes After You Receive A Report

After receiving a report, you do not need to search every abbreviation immediately. Spend 20 minutes doing one thing: turn the report into several clues a clinician can understand and family members can read.

First, check whether warning signs are present. Chest pain, stroke-like symptoms, severe shortness of breath, fainting, altered consciousness, abnormal bleeding, severe pain, or self-harm risk should not wait even if the report looks normal.

Second, mark abnormal items, but do not diagnose. Ask whether this is the first time, or whether the same pattern appeared in the last 1-3 similar reports.

Third, ask whether it is one marker or a group. Blood pressure, lipids, glucose, uric acid, weight and waist, and kidney function often need to be read together.

Fourth, write down recent context. Poor sleep, alcohol, infection, hard exercise, dehydration, menstrual period, pregnancy/postpartum context, stress, medicines, or supplements can all affect interpretation.

Fifth, prepare only three questions: what risk does this suggest, when should it be repeated, and what should trigger earlier care?

These actions are not meant to make you your own clinician. They turn the report from "frightening numbers" into information that can be discussed in a visit.

A useful report summary can be short:

```text
Main abnormalities this time: two lipid markers abnormal, waist increased.

Prior similar results from the last 1-3 reports: slightly high last year, more obvious this year.

Recent context: less sleep, less exercise, weight gain; no chest pain, shortness of breath, or other emergency signs.

Questions for the clinician: What risk do these changes mainly suggest? When should I repeat tests? What should make me seek care earlier?
```

This is not asking ordinary people to judge for clinicians. It turns scattered numbers into a short background that a clinician can understand quickly.

## The Six Most Common Traps

1. **Treating the reference range as a disease boundary.** Reference ranges are not absolute health lines. Different labs, methods, populations, and personal contexts affect interpretation. Outside the range does not always mean disease; inside the range does not always mean no risk.
2. **Treating one result as a long-term trend.** One abnormal value is not a disease, and one normal value is not proof of long-term low risk. Trend matters more than one arrow.
3. **Staring at one marker alone.** Blood pressure, lipids, glucose, uric acid, kidney function, weight, and waist often need to be read as a combination. One number is like one word; the combination is closer to a sentence.
4. **Using a checkup instead of medical care.** When symptoms are present, a checkup is not a substitute for emergency care, a clinic visit, or specialist assessment. A normal report cannot explain away chest pain, stroke-like symptoms, severe shortness of breath, fainting, abnormal bleeding, severe pain, or altered consciousness.
5. **Getting led by "full screening" packages.** More tests are not always better for ordinary people. Useful screening usually depends on target population, evidence, follow-up pathway, and personal risk. If no one knows how to interpret, confirm, or act on a finding, the test may create new anxiety.
6. **Starting supplements or stopping medicines because something is abnormal.** Before starting, stopping, or changing medicines, supplements, herbs, or treatments, ask a clinician or pharmacist. An abnormal report can be a reason for conversation; it should not become a reason for self-adjusting medicines or piling on products.

## When Not To Explain It Yourself

These situations should be brought to a clinician or qualified professional:

- the marker is clearly abnormal, persistently abnormal, or has changed a lot in a short time;
- several cardiovascular, metabolic, or kidney-related markers are abnormal together;
- the person already has diabetes, high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, cancer, immune disease, or another major condition;
- the person is a child, older adult, pregnant/postpartum, or uses several medicines;
- abnormal results come with chest pain, chest pressure, shortness of breath, fainting, altered consciousness, one-sided weakness, slurred speech, severe pain, abnormal bleeding, or self-harm risk;
- the report uses words such as "positive," "suspicious," "mass," "needs further evaluation," or "specialist follow-up recommended," and you do not know the next step.

Words like "positive," "suspicious," or "abnormal" do not always mean a final diagnosis. They often mean repeat testing, additional evaluation, or interpretation together with symptoms, history, and other tests.

## Before The Next Checkup, Do Not Only Ask Whether The Package Is Expensive

Reading a report happens after a checkup. Choosing tests happens before one. They belong in the same chapter, but they are not the same question.

When families choose checkup packages, names often pull them along: basic, upgraded, premium, executive, whole-body, deep scan. The more expensive name seems closer to safety. But a checkup's real value is not filling the package with tests. It is matching tests to this person's risk, and having a clear path after something is found.

Before a checkup, ask only three layers.

The first layer is whether the basic background is clear: height, weight, waist, blood pressure, medicines, prior conditions, family history, lifestyle, and recent symptoms. This background changes how reports are interpreted.

The second layer is whether age- and sex-related screening is appropriate. Blood pressure, diabetes risk, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, cervical cancer, lung cancer screening for high-risk people, osteoporosis, and other topics should not be chosen only by copying what someone else did.

The third layer is whether personal-risk add-ons have a real reason. Symptoms, prior disease, family history, long-term medicines, smoking, obesity, occupational exposure, or chronic disease can change what should be discussed; without a matching risk, "more expensive" does not automatically mean "more reassuring."

Tumor markers, whole-body CT, PET-CT, multi-cancer early detection blood tests, and genetic testing are especially poor fits for the idea that "more means safer." They have their own use cases, and they also bring false positives, over-testing, cost, and downstream decision burden.

For how to choose, start with [Before A Checkup](../../handbook/playbooks/checkup-planning-guide.md). That page separates basic items, age/sex items, and personal-risk add-ons.

## Details Can Be Checked Slowly

There are many checkup markers. What matters most is not memorizing all of them, but first learning the method: trend, combination, background, action.

## Summary

- Checkup markers are not verdicts. They are risk language.
- After receiving a report, look at combinations before arrows.
- In the first 20 minutes, organize warning signs, abnormal items, trends, combinations, recent context, and three clinician questions.
- More checkup items are not automatically better; match them to basic background, age/sex, and personal risk.
- When symptoms or warning signs are present, do not use a checkup report as a substitute for medical care.
